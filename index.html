Why families come

Being there should not mean doing it all yourself.

Three things nearly every parent who contacts us says, more or less word for word.

"I want to be there for it."

Three to eight is the stretch that sets how a person turns out. You do not have to spend it apart from them. You are in the building, twenty steps away, and your child can find you the moment they want you.

"But they need other children."

They do, and that is the honest worry about doing this at home — one child at a table with nobody their own age. Here it is the other way round: a group they see every week, build things with, fall out with, make up with, and grow up alongside.

"I cannot do it all day, every day."

Nobody can be teacher, cook and entertainment for eleven hours, and saying so is not a failing. There is a quiet work zone for you and a programme running for them. Close by, without being on duty every hour.

Why we do it this way

The family is the centre. Everything else is supplemental.

A child learns more from the adults who love them than from anyone who is paid to be with them. That is not a complaint about teachers — it is simply where the weight sits.

Most of the way we arrange childhood ignores that. Parents are separated from their children for the larger part of the child's waking day, and then the outcome is treated as somebody else's department.

We think the parent is the critical part of everything a child learns, and that everything else should support that instead of replacing it. So we build the parts that are hard to do alone: the friends, the trips, the equipment, the specialists, and the other adults who want the same things for their children.

The days out and the facility are both just ways of making that possible.

The days out

Somewhere worth going, with people worth going with.

Most groups like this manage one day a month. We are building it weekly, because friendship needs repetition — for the children and for the adults.

Park days

A different park each time, chosen from the same map we built for Days Out NI. The children run, the adults talk, and everybody goes home tired.

Bus days

A coach hired between us and pointed at somewhere good — the zoo, a castle, a working farm, a city we would not drive to on our own.

Wild days

Beaches, rock pools, forests and rivers. Cold hands, wet socks, and the sort of afternoon a child remembers for twenty years.

Making days

A workshop, a kitchen, a hall with drums in it. Sometimes a specialist we bring in, sometimes a parent who is good at something and wants to share it.

What we are preparing them for

The world our children will live in is not the one we were trained for.

A child starting now will spend their whole working life beside machines that recall everything instantly. Being a slower version of that is not a skill. So we put the weight on the things that hold their value: figuring it out, getting on with people, and finishing what you started.

Open to the best way

We are not a method. Montessori got practical work right. Waldorf got rhythm and materials right. The classical tradition got character right. Forest school got the outdoors right. We take what holds up and leave what does not, and we use AI tools to keep rebuilding the plan around the children actually here.

Tasks, not tests

"Build a remote control car. Now make it get all the way round a loop." Nobody hands them the method. They try, it fails, they change it, it works. That feeling is the lesson — the car is the excuse for it.

Ask your neighbour

In most classrooms that is called cheating. Here it is called working. No adult is ever asked to solve a real problem alone with no help and no resources, so we stop pretending childhood should work that way.

Screens that do not agitate

Reading and number work happen on e-ink tablets — paper-like, no backlight, no glow — for a couple of hours at most. Films go on a projector, watched together. No bank of glowing tablets keeping children quiet.

The facility · in preparation

A park the rain cannot close, and a work desk for you beside it.

Four spaces, each doing one job properly, open 8am to 8pm, seven days. A child moves between them as their day needs — loud to quiet, group to alone, inside to outside and back. And their parent is in the building the whole time.

The barn

High ceilings, soft floors, and the sort of climbing and sliding you would drive an hour to find — indoors. Under seven, a child learns through their body first. So the body comes first.

The garden

Beds to dig, ground to run on, weather to be in. Hours outside on any day that allows it, not fifteen supervised minutes at a fixed time.

The quiet house

Beanbags, low light, books, and a projector for something worth watching together. For the child who has had enough noise, and for the afternoon when everyone has.

The work zone

Quiet, warm, good light, proper internet, good coffee. Most parents work from a laptop now. If you can work anywhere, work twenty steps from your child instead of twenty miles.

What we are actually growing

Three things, on purpose, ahead of everything else.

Reading and numbers come. They always come. These are the things that are much harder to add later, so they get the attention first.

Getting on with people

Mixed ages, small groups, shared jobs, and adults nearby who show how a disagreement actually gets settled. A child who can work with anybody carries that for the whole of their life.

Confidence that is earned

Not praise for existing. The confidence that comes from having done a difficult thing in front of other people and survived it — climbed the high one, led the game, spoke up, made the thing work.

Working it out

Given a goal and no method, most children freeze at first. Within weeks they stop freezing. That switch — from "tell me what to do" to "I will find a way" — is the most useful thing we can hand them.

One table

Real food. This one is not flexible.

Highly processed food, artificial colours, sweeteners and additives are kept out. Not discouraged — kept out, on the days out and in the facility both.

Partly because of what it does to a young body. Mostly because of what it does to a room of young children twenty minutes later, and to the child who then gets told off for something the food did.

Children help make the food. A child who has chopped a carrot has a different relationship with the carrot.

Honestly

It is not for everybody, and it is not trying to be.

A group like this only works if the families in it want the same things. So here it is plainly, before you spend two minutes on an application.

This is for you if

  • You want to be there for your child's growing up, not briefed on it afterwards
  • You want your children around other children whose parents think the same way
  • You want character, confidence and capability ahead of marks on a page
  • You are calm and generous around other people's children
  • You are curious about doing this a genuinely new way
  • Your children are in school, or at home, or somewhere in between — all three are welcome

Probably not for you if

  • What you need is childcare — somewhere to leave them while you are elsewhere
  • You would rather somebody else be accountable for how your child turns out
  • You want your child fed whatever they will accept, for the sake of peace
  • A group where adults are expected to join in does not appeal to you
